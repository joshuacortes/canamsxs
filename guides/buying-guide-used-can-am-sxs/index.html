Can-Am SxS Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

A checklist for new and used buyers — how to read engine hours instead of odometer miles, the wear points that actually predict future repair bills, and the exact questions to ask before you hand over a deposit.

Updated: September 2026 Read time: 12 min Applies to: Maverick R, Maverick X3, Defender, Commander

Quick answer: Before buying any Can-Am SxS — new or used — check the hour meter (not just the odometer), inspect the CVT drive belt and clutch for glazing or cracks, look underneath for frame and skid-plate damage, and confirm the title is clean with a matching VIN. On a used machine, ask for maintenance records and walk away from any seller who can't produce them.

Engine Hours vs. Odometer Miles

Odometer mileage is nearly meaningless on a side-by-side. A machine that's spent 40 hours idling and winching through mud has taken far more wear than one that's covered triple the miles on smooth, wide-open trail. The hour meter — standard on every Can-Am dash — is the number that actually tracks engine and drivetrain use, and it's the figure you should ask for before mileage even comes up.

  • Under 100 hours: Light use. Expect routine wear items only if maintained on schedule.
  • 100–300 hours: Moderate use. This is where belt, brake, and suspension bushing wear typically starts showing up.
  • 300–500 hours: Heavy use. Worth a closer look at the CVT clutch, wheel bearings, and drivetrain components regardless of how clean the machine looks.
  • 500+ hours: High use. Not automatically a dealbreaker, but the price should reflect upcoming major-component wear, and service records matter more than ever at this range.

Hours accumulate differently depending on riding style — rock crawling and mud racking up hours far faster relative to actual mileage than open trail or dune riding. Ask how the machine was used, not just how long it's been owned.

Pre-Purchase Inspection Checklist by System

Walk the machine system by system rather than relying on a general impression. A clean-looking machine can hide expensive problems in the belt housing or underneath the skid plates — these are the areas that actually predict what you'll be paying for in the next year.

1

Engine & Cooling

Check:Start it cold — listen for knocking, excessive smoke, or rough idle before it warms up.
Check:Inspect the radiator and cooling fins for mud packing or bent fins that restrict airflow.
Check:Look for oil residue around the case seams — a sign of gasket or seal wear.
2

CVT & Drive Belt

Check:Pull the belt cover and inspect the belt for glazing, cracking, or uneven wear on the edges.
Check:Feel for excessive play in the primary and secondary clutches when the engine is off.
Check:Ask when the belt was last replaced — this is routine maintenance, not a red flag, but it should be documented.
3

Suspension & Steering

Check:Bounce each corner and listen for clunks or knocks that suggest worn bushings or ball joints.
Check:Inspect shocks for oil leaks or damaged seals — expect wear if the machine has an aggressive suspension package like Smart-Shox.
Check:Turn the wheel lock-to-lock and feel for binding or excessive play in the steering rack.
4

Tires, Wheels & Brakes

Check:Match tread wear across all four corners — uneven wear can point to alignment or suspension issues.
Check:Look for cracked or bent wheels, especially on machines with aftermarket beadlocks.
Check:Inspect brake pad thickness and rotor condition through the wheel spokes.
5

Frame, Skid Plates & Underbody

Check:Look underneath for creased, cracked, or heavily dented skid plates — a strong indicator of hard use.
Check:Check frame rails for rust, cracks, or signs of prior welding repairs.
Check:Inspect CV boots for tears — torn boots let dirt into the joint and accelerate failure.
6

Electrical & Accessories

Check:Test every light, gauge, and switch — including power steering, if equipped, and any driver mode selectors.
Check:Inspect aftermarket wiring for amateur splices, especially around winches or light bars.
Check:Confirm the battery holds a charge and terminals show no corrosion.

Red Flags That Should Walk You Away

Flag

No maintenance records at all. One or two missing receipts is normal; a seller with zero documentation on a 200+ hour machine is a sign it wasn't cared for — or that hours have been reset.

Flag

Mismatched VIN on the title, frame, and any transferable warranty paperwork. This can indicate a rebuilt title, a stolen vehicle, or a frame swap — walk away until it's resolved.

Flag

Fresh paint or undercoating in isolated spots. Selective touch-up, especially on the frame, often hides prior crash or flood damage rather than general upkeep.

Flag

Seller reluctant to let you start the engine cold. A cold start reveals problems a warmed-up engine can mask. Insist on it before any test drive.

Flag

Hour meter that looks inconsistent with wear. Heavily worn pedals, seat bolstering, and grips on a machine reporting under 50 hours is worth questioning directly.

New vs. Used: Which Makes Sense for You

There's no universally right answer here — it comes down to warranty priorities, budget, and how much inspection work you're willing to do yourself. Here's how the two compare on the factors that actually matter:

Factor New Used
Upfront cost Highest, but predictable with financing Lower, with more price variance by condition
Warranty coverage Full factory warranty from day one May have remaining transferable coverage, or none
Inspection risk Minimal — factory-fresh components Requires a thorough system-by-system inspection
Customization Blank slate for upgrades May already include aftermarket parts, for better or worse
Depreciation Steepest in the first 1–2 years Slower going forward — someone else absorbed the initial drop
Best for Buyers who want the latest tech and zero unknowns Buyers comfortable inspecting or paying for a pre-purchase check

Questions to Ask Every Seller

1

How was the machine primarily used? Trail riding, mud, rock crawling, and farm or work use all wear components differently at the same hour count.

2

Do you have service records, and can I see them? Ask for this before you ask about price — it tells you more about the machine's real condition than anything visual.

3

Has it ever been in an accident or rolled? Even a low-speed rollover can bend a frame or damage mounts in ways that aren't obvious on a quick look.

4

What's been replaced or upgraded, and why? A replaced belt or clutch as routine maintenance is normal. A replaced belt after "it made a noise and quit" is worth investigating further.

5

Is there any remaining factory or extended warranty? Warranties on Can-Am SxS models can sometimes transfer to a new owner — confirm in writing, not just verbally.

6

Why are you selling? Not a mechanical question, but the answer often reveals context — an upgrade to a newer model reads very differently than an unexplained quick sale.

Title, VIN, and Recall Checks

Paperwork problems are more expensive to fix than mechanical ones, and they're often invisible during a test ride. Confirm these before any money changes hands:

  • Title status: Confirm it's clean, not salvage or rebuilt, unless you've already priced that risk in.
  • VIN match: The VIN on the title should match the frame stamping and any dealer paperwork exactly.
  • Lien check: Confirm there's no outstanding loan against the machine that could complicate transferring the title.
  • Open recalls: Check the VIN against manufacturer recall records — an unresolved recall is a free fix once you own it, but worth knowing about beforehand.
  • Registration and OHV stickers: Confirm what's required in your state, since requirements for on-road, off-road, and dual-registered use vary.

What Determines Fair Value

Price a used Can-Am against these factors together, not any single one in isolation — a low hour count doesn't offset a rough maintenance history, and a high hour count doesn't automatically mean a bad deal if the records back it up.

  1. Engine hours relative to age. A 3-year-old machine with 60 hours has been babied; the same age with 600 hours has worked for a living.
  2. Documented maintenance history. Full records can justify a price at or near the top of the range for that hour count.
  3. Trim level and factory options. Smart-Shox, Smart-Lok, and higher-output engine packages hold value better than base trims.
  4. Aftermarket additions. Quality upgrades (winches, lighting, protection) can add value; poorly installed ones can subtract it even if the parts themselves are decent.
  5. Regional demand. Machines built for mud or rock terrain often command a premium in regions where that's the dominant riding style, and less elsewhere.
  6. Remaining warranty coverage. A transferable warranty with real time left on it is worth real money — price it in rather than treating it as a bonus.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I trust the odometer or the hour meter on a used SxS?

The hour meter. Odometer mileage doesn't account for idling, winching, or low-speed technical riding, all of which wear the engine and drivetrain without adding much distance. Hours are the standard industry measure for SxS condition.

How many hours is too many on a used Can-Am?

There's no universal cutoff — a well-maintained machine with 500 hours can be a better buy than a neglected one with 150. Past roughly 300 hours, weight documented maintenance more heavily than the hour count itself.

What should I check on the CVT belt before buying?

Pull the belt cover and look for glazing, cracking, or uneven edge wear, and check for play in the primary and secondary clutches. Ask when the belt was last replaced — it's a routine wear item, not automatically a red flag.

Is it safe to buy a used side-by-side without a mechanic's inspection?

It's possible if you work through a thorough system-by-system checklist yourself, but a pre-purchase inspection from a powersports mechanic is worth the cost on any machine over roughly 200 hours or with an incomplete maintenance history.

What paperwork do I need before buying a used Can-Am SxS?

A clean title with a VIN that matches the frame stamping, confirmation there's no outstanding lien, and ideally documented maintenance records. Checking for open manufacturer recalls against the VIN is also worth doing before you finalize the purchase.
